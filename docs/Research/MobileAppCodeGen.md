# Agentic Code Generation Against Existing Production Codebases: Architecture, Limits, and Failure Modes

## The five questions you most need answered (with confidence levels)

1. **Has anyone autonomously shipped net-new features into a production mobile app codebase they did not write?** **No — not autonomously, and not with a published success rate. (High confidence.)** Across every primary source found, no one has documented an AI agent independently shipping a net-new, user-facing feature into a pre-existing production mobile app (React Native/Flutter/Swift/Kotlin) with a reported success rate and quantified human-review level. The closest real brownfield production evidence is GitHub Copilot Coding Agent on `dotnet/runtime` (67.9% PR success rate, all human-requested and human-reviewed) and Cognition's own statement that Devin does brownfield feature work only "when existing code provides clear patterns" — and that was a web app, not mobile. This is *the* gap in the field, and it is precisely where you are operating.

2. **Where is a human still in the loop in every system found?** **At the merge gate, universally. (High confidence.)** Copilot Coding Agent "cannot open PRs on its own" and every PR is human-requested; Devin, Jules, Factory, and Meta's SapFix all route output through mandatory human review before landing. No production system auto-merges agent output to trunk. Cognition frames it precisely: Devin is "senior-level at codebase understanding but junior at execution" — you "assign it tasks a junior developer would complete in 4–8 hrs of work, verify the pull request, and scale the workload horizontally."

3. **What is the measured, real-world success rate for agentic modification of an existing codebase?** **Roughly 65–70% PR merge rate on well-scoped brownfield tasks with human review, falling below 20% on genuinely hard/enterprise codebases. (High confidence on the range, medium on generalizability.)** Devin: 67% of PRs merged (up from 34% a year earlier, per Cognition's Nov-2025 performance review); Copilot CCA on `dotnet/runtime`: 67.9% (535 of 878 PRs merged). These are *with* a human requesting and reviewing every task. On the private/commercial SWE-bench Pro set, the best model scores under 20%.

4. **Is the additive/modifying distinction load-bearing across the field?** **Yes, overwhelmingly. (High confidence.)** Every working system whose safety story we could verify depends on output being separable from the host: sandboxed micro-apps (Gigacatalyst), extension points and managed packages (Salesforce, ServiceNow, SAP), plugins (Atlassian Forge, Jack Henry Banno, Q2 Caliper), and deterministic recipe-based transforms (OpenRewrite). The systems that *do* modify existing surfaces (Devin, Copilot CCA) succeed only on bounded, pattern-replicable edits and are gated by human review. Nobody has a proven safety story for autonomous modification of a host's existing screens.

5. **What does this cost per run, and how do companies price?** **Wide range: from ~$1.30 to ~$150 per benchmark task; consumer agents $20–$200/month; enterprise is custom/opaque. (High confidence on published figures.)** Aider polyglot runs range from ~$1.30 (DeepSeek V3.2) to ~$146 (o3-pro high) per full benchmark. Devin is $20/mo (Pro) to $200/mo (Max) plus usage; SWE-bench Verified single-model evaluations have cost from ~$5 to ~$1,800. Gigacatalyst, Factory, and most enterprise players publish no pricing.

## TL;DR

- **No one has solved your exact problem.** Autonomous, net-new feature generation into a large, human-written *mobile* codebase you can't write to, maintained as a continuously re-derived fork — that specific combination has no published precedent. The field's entire safety story rests on the additive/separable-by-construction principle you are already using for visual-only changes; the moment you extend to modifying existing screens, you leave the safe zone that every incumbent designed around.
- **The measured reality is a ~65–70% merge rate with a human on every task.** Best-in-class brownfield agents (Devin at 67%, Copilot CCA at 67.9% on a real production repo) succeed on bounded, pattern-following edits and degrade sharply with cross-file coupling and codebase size — success falls below 10% once a fix touches 3+ files or 100+ lines, and below 5% in repos over 500 files. Mobile is meaningfully harder than web and is barely benchmarked at all.
- **Gigacatalyst's claims are directionally supported but self-reported, and the company has visibly repositioned** from "customers build features in-app" (YC launch, May 2026) toward "sales/CS teams configure demos and POCs" (site, mid-2026). Its architecture is sandboxed micro-apps built *on the host's APIs* with no host-codebase changes — the opposite of your fork-and-modify approach. Its one named customer is UpKeep (Ryan Chan, CEO), claiming "over 1,000 UpKeep customers use it daily."

## Key Findings

**Gigacatalyst is an API-composition sandbox, not a codebase-modification system.** It parses the host's APIs and generates micro-apps that run *beside* the product in isolated sandboxes, inheriting the host's auth/RBAC. It explicitly makes "no changes to the host codebase." This is architecturally the inverse of your approach and shares almost none of your hard problems (and none of your safety guarantees around modifying existing surfaces). The company launched at YC Spring 2026 (batch labeled S26/P26), solo founder Namanyay Goel, ~4 employees.

**The additive-vs-modifying distinction is the single most load-bearing idea in the entire field.** Decades of multi-tenant SaaS architecture (Salesforce metadata, ServiceNow, SAP's "keep the core clean" doctrine, Shopify, Atlassian Forge, fintech plugin SDKs) converged independently on extension points and sandboxes for one reason: per-tenant source modification makes upgrades combinatorially expensive and unsafe. You are proposing to do the thing the entire industry spent 20 years engineering *around*.

**Measured brownfield success rates cluster at ~65–70% with mandatory human review**, collapsing on hard tasks. Benchmarks and production data agree that the failure surface is cross-file coupling, size, and semantic (compiles-fine-but-wrong) regressions.

**Automated fork maintenance against moving upstream is a solved *deterministic* problem (OpenRewrite, Piranha, Mergiraf) and an unsolved *semantic* one.** Syntax-aware merge is mature; detecting conflicts that merge cleanly but break behavior is still a research problem. No one has shipped an agent that autonomously maintains a long-lived semantic fork.

**Mobile is the worst-supported case in the field.** iOS/Swift has essentially no robust agent benchmark; native build config, code signing, and App Store review add failure modes web builders never face.

### Part 3 — The brownfield question (most important)

**Benchmark performance** *(measured — leaderboards & arXiv)*:

- **SWE-bench Verified**: frontier models now score ~70–81%. As of April 2026, leading scores cluster around 80–81% (e.g., Claude Opus 4.5/4.6-class and Gemini 3.x-class models in the ~80% range per independent trackers). But this benchmark is largely Python, largely single-repo bug-fixing with a known human fix, and is considered near-saturated by researchers.
- **SWE-bench Pro** (Scale AI, arXiv:2509.16941): far harder. Best models ~43.6% (Claude Sonnet 4.5) on the public set; **"the best models score less than 20% in the commercial set"** — Claude Opus 4.1 drops 22.7%→17.8%, GPT-5 falls 23.1%→14.9%. This is the single best signal that *enterprise* brownfield is dramatically harder than open-source benchmarks suggest. Tasks average ~107 lines of change across 4.1 files; Python scores highest, JS/TS lowest.
- **Aider polyglot** (225 exercises across C++, Go, Java, JS, Python, Rust): top score **88.0% (GPT-5 high)**, then GPT-5 medium 86.7%, o3-pro high 84.9%, Gemini 2.5 Pro 83.1%, Grok-4 high 79.6%, DeepSeek V3.2 74.2%. Cost per full run ranges from ~$1.30 (DeepSeek V3.2) to ~$146 (o3-pro high). In ~15 months the frontier rose from 61.7% (o1, Dec 2024) to 88% — a ~26-point jump.
- **Commit0** (build a library from scratch to pass tests): GPT-5.2-Codex ~50–62.5%; notably, some models lost 30+ points when a git-history data-leakage vulnerability was patched — a caution about benchmark integrity.
- **Terminal-Bench**: Factory's Code Droid claimed #1 at 58.75% with Claude Opus 4.1 (vendor-reported).
- **METR time-horizon** (Kwa et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.14499): the 50%-success task length that frontier agents can complete has doubled roughly every 7 months (accelerating to ~4 months in 2024–25), rising "from 4 seconds in 2019 to more than 16 hours in 2026." **Crucial caveat:** this is the *50%* reliability horizon; the *80%* horizon is roughly 5× shorter, and agents "succeed less than 10% of the time on tasks taking humans more than approximately four hours." The reliability level needed for autonomous production work is therefore far below the headline figure.

**Production performance** *(measured — engineering blogs & one arXiv)*:

- **Cognition Devin** (Cognition's Nov-2025 "Devin's 2025 Performance Review"): **"67% of its PRs are now merged vs 34% last year," "4x faster at problem solving and 2x more efficient in resource consumption."** Deployed at Goldman Sachs, Santander, Nubank. Devin does brownfield features only "when existing code provides clear patterns" and "can't independently tackle an ambiguous coding project end-to-end like a senior engineer could." Test coverage at customers "typically rises from 50–60% to 80–90%." Named-customer merge rates (vendor-reported): Gumroad/antiwork >85% (1,583 PRs), The Citation Group 80% (271 PRs, "after senior engineer review"). Nubank's Talend/ETL migration (6M LOC, ~100k data-class implementations) is cited at "8–12x efficiency" and ">20x cost savings" — efficiency claims, not merge rates.
- **GitHub Copilot Coding Agent on `dotnet/runtime`** (Microsoft .NET blog, "Ten Months with CCA in dotnet/runtime," May 2025–Mar 2026 — the strongest real brownfield datapoint): **67.9% success rate** — "878 CCA pull requests in dotnet/runtime alone (535 merged, representing over 95,000 lines of code added and 31,000 lines removed)," 253 closed. Success rose from 41.7% in month one to ~71%. "Every CCA PR was created at the explicit request of a human with maintainer rights; CCA cannot open PRs on its own." Task-type variance: cleanup/removal highest at 84.7%, performance tasks hardest at 54.5%. Notably only **3 of 535 merged PRs were reverted (0.6%, vs a 0.8% human baseline)** — i.e., what does merge is about as safe as human work. 56% of merged CCA PRs touched a test file (vs 38% for humans). Human baseline in the same repo: 85–87%.
- **Microsoft org-wide CLI-agent study** (Murphy-Hill, Butler & Savelieva, arXiv:2607.01418, July 2026; tens of thousands of engineers, Claude Code + GitHub Copilot CLI, early-2026 rollout): adopters "merged roughly 24% more pull requests than they would have otherwise." The authors caution: "We use merged pull requests as our proxy for output — acknowledging that a merged PR is not the same as the value it delivers."
- **Factory.ai / Google Jules**: no published production merge-rate percentage could be found for either. Jules reached GA at Google I/O (May 2026) and does not auto-merge.
- **Devin early independent test** (Answer.AI, 2024): 3 of 20 tasks succeeded (~15%) — a reminder of how far real-world numbers were from the launch demo, and how much they depend on task scoping.

**The automated large-scale change lineage (closest real precedent):**

- **Moderne/OpenRewrite** *(measured)*: deterministic, compiler-accurate transforms on a **Lossless Semantic Tree** (type-aware AST that preserves formatting/comments). 10,000+ recipes, 10+ languages. Explicitly *rules-based, not probabilistic*: "the same fix lands identically whether it touches one repository or 100,000." This is the gold standard for *safe, repeatable* brownfield change — and it works precisely because it is deterministic and semantic, not LLM free-generation. ($30M Series B; used across Fortune 500.)
- **Uber Piranha** *(measured)*: AST-based stale-feature-flag deletion for Objective-C/Swift/Java; removed ~2,000 stale flags across Android/iOS; generates a diff in <3 minutes on average; **the diff is assigned to the flag's author for human review before landing.** Even a narrow, deterministic, single-purpose tool keeps a human at the merge gate.
- **Meta SapFix/Getafix** *(measured)*: Getafix learns fix templates from past human fixes (top-1 accuracy 12–91% by bug category); SapFix generates patches (template or AST-mutation), validates by compiling + running existing and Sapienz-generated tests, and **proposes to a human reviewer — "not designed to deploy fixes to production code on its own."** In a 90-day pilot it generated 165 patches for 57 crashes; median 69 minutes from detection to human-reviewable fix, across 6 apps (Facebook, Messenger, Instagram, FBLite, Workplace, Workchat) totaling tens of millions of LOC. This is the closest large-scale precedent to automated change on *mobile* code, and it is (a) bug-fixing, not net-new features, and (b) human-gated.
- **Google Rosie / large-scale changes**: deterministic, tooling-driven, human-reviewed shards. Same pattern.

**Failure taxonomy** *(measured + inferred)*: The published evidence maps almost exactly onto your predicted list:
- **Cross-file coupling & size** (from SWE-bench "Goes Live!", arXiv:2505.23419): a single-file patch changing <5 lines is solved ~48% of the time; success falls below 10% once a patch touches 3+ files or spans 100+ lines; "patches that touch seven or more files are never solved"; repos over 500 files "rarely exceed 5%," while repos under 100 files / 20k LOC "often yield success rates above twenty per-cent."
- **Semantic (compiles-fine) regressions**: SWE-bench Pro notes larger models "fail on semantic or algorithmic correctness in large, multi-file edits." Semantic merge conflicts (clean merge, broken behavior) are an explicitly unsolved research area.
- **Repository-specific difficulty**: some repos yield <10% for all models; documentation quality and complexity dominate.
- **Language variance**: Python highest; JavaScript/TypeScript consistently lowest — directly relevant since React Native is TS/JS.
- **Non-determinism**: benchmark runs vary run-to-run; the Holistic Agent Leaderboard runs multiple trials specifically because of this.
- **Edge/empty/loading states and implicit invariants**: under-tested by benchmarks (which check pass-to-pass tests) but flagged repeatedly by practitioners as where agents silently break behavior.

**Mobile-specific difficulty** *(measured — new 2026 benchmarks)*: Mobile is meaningfully harder and barely measured:
- **MobileDev-Bench** (2026) is the first benchmark spanning production mobile apps across Android Native, React Native, and Flutter; it explicitly captures "multi-artifact fixes… coordinated changes across heterogeneous project components" — the coupling that agents fail on. Issue descriptions average 308 tokens (device configs, repro steps, platform versions) — more context to get right.
- **React Native Evals** (Callstack) focuses on RN-specific delivery (navigation, animation, async state) because general benchmarks "do not tell you enough about whether a model understands the quirks of a production mobile stack… easy to get almost right but still ship broken UX."
- **iOS/Swift** has essentially no robust agent benchmark (the best is ~28 handcrafted examples from May 2025, already stale, per practitioner Vivian Qu).
- Native build config, code signing, platform-conditional code, and App Store review are failure modes web builders never touch. **Verdict: yes, agentic modification of mobile codebases is harder than web, and the tooling/evidence base is years behind.**

**Died or pivoted** *(measured)*:
- **Builder.ai** (backed by Microsoft and the Qatar Investment Authority; ~$1.5B peak valuation, ~$450M raised): filed insolvency in May 2025 after lender Viola Credit seized funds; reporting alleged the "Natasha" AI-builds-your-app assistant's work "was done by about 700 engineers, mostly based in India." The canonical cautionary tale for this exact category.
- **CodeParrot** (YC W23): Figma→production frontend code; shut down July 2025 — "the generated code wasn't reliable enough for production. Teams still had to spend time fixing outputs, which erased the value." **This is your risk in one sentence.**
- **Tome**: pivoted away from AI presentations entirely (70 employees → 6).
- Broader pattern (Google Cloud's Darren Mowry, Feb 2026): "thin IP wrapped around Gemini or GPT-5" faces extinction; "when Anthropic launched Claude Code, it made some AI coding tool integrations less defensible." Aggregate AI-startup failure figures (40%+ within 24 months) circulate widely but come from secondary trackers and should be treated as indicative, not precise.

### Part 4 — Agent-maintained forks against moving upstream

- **Deterministic downstream-fork maintenance is mature but expensive** *(measured)*: Chromium downstream forks (Edge, Brave, Opera, Vivaldi) must "merge and ship upstream fixes on their own release cadence," creating a structural lag between upstream fix and downstream ship. Reviewers note that "feature-rich UIs… increase maintenance burden and the chance of delayed upstream merges" — the more you diverge, the worse the rebase tax. Android OEM/AOSP and Linux distro patch queues are the same story: patch-carrying at scale is a solved-but-costly discipline, and the cost scales with divergence.
- **Syntax-aware merge is solved; semantic merge is not** *(measured)*: **Mergiraf** (Rust, 25–33 languages) parses files to grammar-level syntax trees and merges structurally, cleanly resolving "false conflicts" (e.g., independent import additions) that line-based Git can't — but falls back to conflict markers on genuine structural conflicts. Microsoft holds patents on ML/transformer-based three-way merge resolution. **The hard, unsolved part is semantic conflict detection** — changes that merge cleanly but break behavior. Academic work (Rover/LLM-based resolution, RefFilter refactoring-aware static analysis, "semantic conflict-freedom" formalizations) is active research, not shipping product. An empirical study of 131,154 conflict resolutions across 10,000 GitHub projects found that only a fraction fit a small set of language-agnostic auto-resolution patterns.
- **Has anyone shipped an agent that autonomously maintains a long-lived fork?** *(inferred — negative)*: **No published example found.** The deterministic tools (OpenRewrite, Mergiraf) are not agents; the agents (Devin, Copilot) do one-shot tasks with human review, not continuous autonomous re-derivation of a fork against moving upstream. There is no published drift-rate or maintenance-burden data for an autonomous fork-maintaining agent because no such system is publicly documented. **This is genuinely novel territory in your architecture.**

### Part 5 — Per-tenant customization in multi-tenant SaaS (incumbents)

- **Why everyone converges on extension points + sandboxes** *(measured)*: **Salesforce's** metadata-driven multitenant kernel stores *tenant-specific metadata*, not tenant-specific source; a single shared kernel "reads metadata and data to dynamically provide tenant-specific applications… at runtime," letting "Salesforce, tenants, and ISVs independently evolve their portions of the system without interference." Managed packages add namespacing and IP protection. **ServiceNow, SAP** (explicit "in-app vs side-by-side" / keep-the-core-clean doctrine), **Shopify** (Functions/Extensions/Hydrogen), **Atlassian Forge**, **Slack/Zendesk** apps, **Retool Embed**, and embedded-analytics vendors (Sisense, Looker, Qlik) all do the same. **Fintech:** **Q2 Caliper SDK** (2018) exposes "the same tools Q2 developers have consumed internally for more than seven years" so FIs "build their own features on top of the Q2 Platform"; **Jack Henry Banno Plugin Framework** lets institutions build sandboxed web "plugins" (cards) rendered inside Banno Online/Mobile via a JavaScript bridge.
- **The specific failure they are all avoiding:** per-tenant source forks make every upstream change an N-way merge-and-retest problem, turning maintenance cost into O(tenants × changes) and making platform-wide upgrades unsafe and eventually impossible. Metadata/extension models keep the shared core singular and upgradable while divergence lives in isolated, versioned, sandboxed artifacts. **This is exactly the cost your architecture re-introduces** — you are choosing per-tenant forks, the thing every mature platform engineered away.
- **Q2 Code (April 16, 2026) — your buyer building in-house** *(measured — press release)*: Q2 Holdings announced **Q2 Code**, "a governed AI-powered development environment," built by integrating **Anthropic's Claude Code via Amazon Bedrock**, that turns natural-language prompts into **"Q2 SDK-compliant extensions and integrations"** on the Q2 Digital Banking Platform, via Q2 Innovation Studio. **Mid-Hudson Valley Federal Credit Union** is the named early-access customer; the Early Access program "will expand throughout 2026… ahead of broader availability." **This is decisive for your build-vs-buy risk:** a platform is building the AI-customization layer itself — and, tellingly, it is doing so *on top of its existing SDK/extension model* (governed, SDK-compliant extensions), **not** by letting AI modify the host source. Even the platform building this in-house respects the additive boundary.

### Part 6 — Visual validation state of the art

- **Visual regression tooling** *(measured/vendor)*: Percy, Chromatic, Applitools, and Playwright/Puppeteer screenshot diffing are mature. The perennial problem is **flakiness/false positives** from anti-aliasing, dynamic content, fonts, and timing; teams manage it with visual-AI tolerance (Applitools), baseline approval workflows, and ignore-regions — i.e., a human still adjudicates ambiguous diffs.
- **VLM-as-judge for UI quality** *(measured — arXiv)*: Correlation with human judgment is moderate-to-strong but not reliable enough to run unsupervised:
  - On **Design2Code-HARD** (UI2Code^N, arXiv:2511.08195), a VLM judge reproduced the human ranking of model outputs with Pearson r = 0.93 on net-win margins (and Spearman 1.0 on ordering), but with "consistently more conservative preference magnitudes."
  - UI-to-code / design work: best judges reach r ≈ 0.83 at the design-instance level, "approaching the 85% GPT-4–human pairwise agreement reported by MT-Bench."
  - But **on the general MLLM-as-a-Judge benchmark, state-of-the-art VLM judges achieve only 32–34% exact agreement with human ratings**, and GPT-4V reaches ~70% human agreement overall (79% on pairwise). A 2026 paper concludes bluntly: **"VLM Judges Can Rank but Cannot Score."** Even top VLMs "lag expert human judges by 15–20% on complex tasks (e.g., dynamic code+UI evaluation)."
  - **Implication for your accept condition:** a VLM judging screenshots is a decent *relative/ranking* signal and a decent gate for gross defects, but it is unreliable as an *absolute* "is this good/correct" oracle — exactly the judgment your autonomous accept condition needs. Pairwise ("is B at least as good as A?") is more reliable than scoring.
- **Bounding the generate-validate-regenerate loop** *(inferred + measured)*: In practice loops are bounded by hard iteration caps, cost/credit caps (Replit's agent burning credits fixing its own mistakes is the cautionary example), and — critically — a human as the final termination condition. No system found bounds an *unbounded* visual loop with a VLM judge alone; VLM ranking is used to *select* among candidates, with a cap and a human backstop.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Hold the line on visual-only until the safety story is proven.** Your current scope (arrangement changes, API surface provably identical, separable-by-construction) is the *only* part of your architecture that matches a proven-safe pattern in the field. Everything the industry has de-risked lives on the additive side of the line. Do not cross into modifying existing screens until you have the gates below.

**Stage 1 — Instrument the fork-drift cost before you commit to net-new features.** Run your agent-maintained-fork re-derivation against a real, actively-shipping upstream mobile repo for 8–12 weeks and measure: (a) rebase frequency, (b) % of merges that are clean vs. conflicted, (c) % of clean merges that introduce a *behavioral* regression caught only by tests/screenshots (your semantic-conflict rate), and (d) engineer-minutes per re-derivation. **Threshold to proceed:** if semantic regressions on clean merges exceed a few percent, or maintenance burden grows super-linearly with upstream velocity, the fork model won't scale and you should pivot toward an extension/sandbox model like every incumbent.

**Stage 2 — For net-new features, adopt the deterministic-scaffold + constrained-generation pattern, not free-code generation.** The evidence is unambiguous: deterministic, AST/LST-based, semantic transforms (OpenRewrite, Piranha) are safe at scale; free LLM generation into large codebases merges at ~67% *with a human on every task* and collapses on cross-file work. Constrain your agent to (a) additive files/modules wherever possible, (b) a template/registry with compile + type + schema gates, and (c) provable API-surface invariance as you already do. Treat any change touching 3+ existing files as high-risk by default (benchmark success <10% there).

**Stage 3 — Keep a human at the merge gate, and say so.** Every production system found — Copilot CCA, Devin, SapFix, Piranha — gates on human review, and your buyers (banks, via Q2 Code) are building governed, SDK-compliant, human-reviewed pipelines. "Fully autonomous" is a promise no one else keeps; positioning yourself as *autonomous generation with a governed human/UAT gate* (which your `uat`-branch design already implies) is both more honest and more sellable. Note that Copilot CCA's *merged* output was as safe as humans (0.6% revert vs 0.8%) — the value of the gate is in filtering the ~32% that shouldn't merge, not in distrusting what passes. **Benchmark to relax the gate:** only move toward more autonomy on a change class once its measured clean-merge-without-regression rate on your own corpus clears a high bar (aim for the 95%+ range that deterministic tools achieve, not the ~67% that free-generation achieves).

**Stage 4 — Use VLM-as-judge for ranking and gross-defect gating, never as the sole accept oracle.** Use pairwise VLM comparison to select among candidate renders and to catch obvious breakage; pair it with deterministic visual-regression diffing (Percy/Applitools-style) and a hard iteration+cost cap; keep a human backstop for absolute "is this correct" judgments where VLM scoring is unreliable (32–34% exact agreement is not an oracle).

**Stage 5 — Watch the build-vs-buy threat directly.** Q2 Code is the leading indicator: platforms will build governed AI customization in-house, on their SDKs. **Decision trigger:** if two or more of your target platforms ship a native AI-extension layer, your wedge as an external, no-write-access fork-maintainer erodes fast — pivot to being the *engine/tooling* underneath their governed layer, or to platforms too small to build it themselves.

## Caveats

- **Confidence varies by section.** Gigacatalyst internals are largely *inferred* from marketing surfaces and the founder's blogs; no job postings, changelogs, or engineering deep-dives were found to confirm the sandbox boundary (process vs. container vs. iframe) or the auth mechanism (token passthrough vs. OIDC vs. session proxy). Treat all Gigacatalyst architecture claims as vendor-claimed-plus-inference.
- **All Gigacatalyst traction numbers are self-reported** and appear only on the company's own pages and reprinting AI directories. One named customer (UpKeep) does not corroborate aggregate figures.
- **Benchmark ≠ production.** SWE-bench Verified ~80% is a near-saturated, Python-heavy, single-repo bug-fix benchmark; it does *not* predict performance on your mobile, cross-file, net-new-feature case. The honest production analog is ~67% merge with human review, <20% on hard enterprise code.
- **Some aggregate/secondary figures are soft.** AI-startup failure percentages, and a few leaderboard rankings for very recent models (e.g., specific Claude Opus 4.5/4.6 Aider placements), come from secondary trackers and conflict across sources; where they conflict I have favored primary leaderboards and dated the frontier at GPT-5-high 88% on Aider polyglot.
- **Model capability is a moving target.** METR's doubling trend means the brownfield numbers here may improve materially within quarters — but the *80%+-reliability* horizon needed for true autonomy lags the 50% horizon substantially, and mobile/semantic-conflict problems are not obviously on the fast part of that curve.

## What this means for the architecture at the top

**Where you are harder than everything found:**
- **You want autonomy where everyone else keeps a human at the merge gate.** Devin, Copilot CCA, SapFix, Piranha — all human-gated. Your promise of autonomous operation is the thing no production system delivers. Your `uat`-branch landing is your saving grace: lean into it as the governed gate, not a compromise.
- **You are brownfield, mobile, and net-new-feature — the three hardest axes simultaneously.** Benchmarks say brownfield cross-file edits succeed <10%; mobile is the worst-supported domain (no iOS benchmark; RN is TS/JS, the lowest-scoring language); and net-new features are harder than the bug-fixes that constitute nearly all measured production success.
- **You are re-introducing the per-tenant fork cost the entire industry engineered away**, and doing it *autonomously against moving upstream* — combining the Chromium-fork rebase tax with the unsolved semantic-conflict problem, with no human on the merge by design.
- **No write access removes your ability to add extension points.** Incumbents made per-tenant customization safe by building *extension points into the host*. You cannot; you must modify a fork of code you don't own, which is strictly harder than what Salesforce/Q2/Jack Henry do.

**Where you are easier:**
- **Your visual-only scope is genuinely in the safe zone.** Provable API-surface invariance and separable-by-construction changes match the one pattern the field has de-risked. If you can keep net-new features *additive* (new screens/modules that don't modify existing surfaces), you inherit that safety story — this is exactly what Gigacatalyst, Salesforce, and Q2 Code all do.
- **You control the base and design forks and re-derive deterministically.** Unlike a one-shot agent facing an unfamiliar repo cold, you can build persistent structural understanding of the upstream (the "project brain" idea from Goel's own Giga work) and amortize it across re-derivations.
- **The `uat` gate gives you a natural, buyer-legible human checkpoint** that turns "autonomous but risky" into "autonomous generation, governed acceptance" — the exact posture banks are buying from Q2 Code.
- **Deterministic tooling is available to do the dangerous parts safely.** OpenRewrite-style LST transforms and Mergiraf-style structural merge can handle the mechanical re-derivation; reserve the LLM for the genuinely generative, additive parts and gate everything with compile/type/schema/screenshot checks. Your safest path is *deterministic scaffold + constrained additive generation + governed UAT gate*, not free-code generation into existing screens.
